Lesson 1

The role of the manager

A manager is a person who supports, activates, and is responsible for the work of others. Managers exist in all organizations: businesses, schools, hospitals, sports clubs, charities.

The three levels of management

Top-level: Strategy & VisionCEO · COO · CFO · CMO · CTO · President · VP · Board of Directors
Middle-level: Implementation & CoordinationDepartment, Regional, Plant, Branch, General, Operations, Project Managers · Senior Supervisor
Lower-level (first-line): Execution & SupervisionSupervisor · Team Leader · Shift Manager · Foreman · Retail Store Manager · Assistant/Office Manager
Top

Sets the direction

  • Company goals and long-term vision
  • Major financial and strategic decisions
  • Oversees middle and lower managers
  • Responsible for overall success and growth
Middle

The bridge

  • Turns top-level strategy into operational plans
  • Supervises and mentors lower managers
  • Coordinates between departments
  • Reports performance metrics upward
Lower

Runs the day

  • Supervises daily tasks and workflows
  • Trains and onboards frontline staff
  • Makes sure quality standards and deadlines are met
  • Reports problems to middle management
Exam tip · 100% score alert

Classify by what the person does, not by the word "manager" in the title. A "Retail Store Manager" supervises frontline staff day to day, so they are lower-level. A "Regional Manager" coordinates several stores, so they are middle-level.

Six types of managers

Pair 1

Line manager vs. Staff manager

Line: directly supervises the day-to-day work of employees who make the organization's goods or services (e.g., production supervisor).

Staff: uses specialist expertise to advise and support line workers (e.g., Human Resources, Legal).

Pair 2

Functional manager vs. General manager

Functional: responsible for one area such as finance, marketing, production, personnel, accounting or sales.

General: responsible for complex, multifunctional units; coordinates across several departments.

Pair 3

Project manager vs. Executive manager

Project: coordinates people and resources to finish a specific project on time and on budget. Often temporary.

Executive: responsible for the whole organization or a major part of it; sets long-term direction and culture (e.g., CEO, CFO).

Daniel Katz's Skills Model

■ Conceptual skills

Think analytically, solve complex problems, see the organization as a whole system.

■ Human skills

Work well with people, build trust, communicate. Vital at all levels.

■ Technical skills

Specialist know-how to do specific tasks (accounting, coding, engineering).

Good vs. bad managers (and employees)

Good manager

Approachable, fair, confident but admits mistakes. Communicates clearly, listens actively, gives constructive feedback, leads by example.

Bad manager

Arrogant, biased (favouritism), insecure. Micromanages, blames others, withholds information, creates a toxic or fearful environment.

Good employee

Responsible, honest, motivated. On time, meets deadlines, takes initiative, works well in a team.

Bad employee

Lazy, negative, unreliable. Often late or absent, misses deadlines, complains, disengaged.

Lesson 2

The four functions of management (POLC)

Management is the process of achieving organizational goals through four functions that run as a continuous cycle. Tap each one to see what it looks like in a real workplace.

Exam tip · Planning vs. Controlling

Planning sets the target before the work. Controlling checks results after (or during) the work against that target and takes corrective action. Key words for Controlling: measure, compare, monitor, KPI, corrective action. Then the cycle loops back to Planning.

Lesson 3

Classical management theories

Classical theory came from the late 1800s, during the Industrial Revolution. It assumed employees have only economic and physical needs (social needs and job satisfaction don't matter) and searched for one universal "best way" to do every job, to raise efficiency.

Scientific

F.W. Taylor

The "one best way" to do a job. Observe, measure and record jobs to find the most efficient method.

Administrative

Henri Fayol

Management as a universal process (planning, organizing, controlling, deciding) built on 14 principles.

Bureaucratic

Max Weber

Rules, guidelines and division of labour. Success depends on the legal power of the position, not the person's personality.

Scientific Management: F.W. Taylor (1856–1915)

An American engineer who started as a machinist, invented work-study and founded the scientific approach. He saw humans as machines to be optimised, and treated money as the main motivator.

  • Piecework pay: paid per item produced (plus bonuses), not a flat wage.
  • Extreme division of labour: each worker does one narrow, repetitive task, learned quickly and done fast.
  • Tight management control: work goes at the pace of the factory/manager, not the employee.
  • Goals: improve efficiency, reduce costs, increase wages (higher efficiency → profit → pay), remove guesswork.

Taylor's four principles

  1. Science, not rule of thumb: standard methods from observation and measurement.
  2. Scientific selection and training of workers by skill.
  3. Cooperation between managers and workers with clear responsibilities.
  4. Division of work and responsibility: managers plan, workers execute.

Impact: shaped mass production at Ford Motor Company. Criticism: treated workers like machines, put speed over well-being, led to poor industrial relations and the growth of trade unions.

Administrative Management: Henri Fayol's 14 principles

Fayol (1841–1925) believed every business should follow these principles. A memory trick for the first five: "Do All Duties Under Unity" (Division, Authority, Discipline, Unity of command, Unity of direction).

#PrincipleIn plain words
1Division of workSpecialising makes workers more efficient.
2Authority (and responsibility)Managers have the right to give orders and are responsible for results.
3DisciplineEmployees respect rules and agreements.
4Unity of commandEach employee takes orders from one boss only.
5Unity of directionActivities with the same goal follow one plan under one manager.
6Subordination of individual interestThe organization's goals come before personal interests.
7RemunerationFair pay for work done.

Your slides asked you to research these yourself, so the list above follows Fayol's standard 14. Are they still relevant? Mostly yes: unity of command, fair pay, equity and team spirit still guide modern firms, though strict centralization fits less well in fast-moving companies.

Exam tip · Unity of command vs. unity of direction

Command = one boss per employee. Direction = one plan per goal. Students mix these up every year.

Bureaucratic Management: Max Weber

Weber (1864–1920) argued organizations run best on clear rules and legal-rational authority: you obey a manager because of the position they hold, not because they are charming.

  • Clear division of labour and defined job duties
  • A hierarchy of authority (chain of command)
  • Formal written rules and procedures
  • Impersonality: rules apply equally to everyone
  • Hiring and promotion based on qualifications and merit
  • Careful written records

Strengths: consistency, fairness, predictability. Weaknesses: "red tape", slow decisions, rigid, can discourage creativity. Governments, banks and the military are classic examples.

Critique of classical theory

  • Ignores social needs: doesn't think social needs matter to workers.
  • Treats everyone the same: ignores that employees differ.
  • Excludes employee voice: ignores workers' ideas about how work gets done.
Exam tip

A question that says "relies on legal authority, not personality" = Bureaucratic (Weber). "One best way" = Scientific (Taylor). "14 principles" or "universal process of managing" = Administrative (Fayol).

Lesson 4

Modern management theories

McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Two opposite sets of assumptions a manager holds about employees. Each assumption leads to a different management style.

Theory XTheory Y
Employees are…Naturally lazy, avoid work, don't want responsibility, fear changeLike work, seek responsibility, show initiative and creativity, excited by change
The manager is…Authoritarian, repressive, tight control, no employee involvement, pessimisticLiberating, empowering, gives responsibility and freedom to develop, optimistic
Workers become…Unmotivated, lazy, rebellious, poor workMotivated, loyal, productive, proud of their work, respect managers
Exam tip · Self-fulfilling prophecy

Whichever theory a manager believes, they turn out to be "right", because the way they manage causes employees to act that way. If asked "what effect do a manager's assumptions have?", the answer is a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Three modern approaches

Approach 1

Quantitative

Uses maths models, statistics and decision tools.

+ data-driven, optimises resources, reduces risk via forecasting
− ignores human factors, not all problems are measurable

Best for: large-scale operations, manufacturing, logistics, finance, stable demand (e.g., banking).

Approach 2

Systems

The business is a set of interconnected parts (departments, employees, suppliers) working to common goals.

+ cross-department teamwork, keeps parts in sync, holistic problem-solving
− complex, time-consuming coordination

Best for: large, multi-department corporations.

Approach 3

Contingency

No one-size-fits-all; the right decision depends on the situation.

+ flexible, works across industries, encourages innovation
− needs skilled decision-makers, can be inconsistent

Best for: startups, fast-changing industries (tech, fashion).

Final verdict from your slides: the Contingency Approach is often best overall, because it combines parts of the Quantitative and Systems approaches while staying flexible.

Lesson 5

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

CSR is an organization's obligation to act in ways that serve both its own interests and the interests of society. A stakeholder is any individual, group or organization that can affect or be affected by a business's decisions, actions and policies.

Inside the business

Internal stakeholders

  • Employees: fair pay, safety, respect for rights
  • Managers & executives: performance, ethics, reputation
  • Owners & shareholders: profits plus responsible, sustainable behaviour
Outside the business

External stakeholders

  • Customers: safe quality products, honest marketing
  • Suppliers & partners: fair contracts, on-time payment
  • Governments: compliance with labour, environmental, tax laws
  • Local communities: jobs, noise, pollution, investment
  • NGOs & activists: monitor and pressure firms
  • Trade unions: workers' wages, safety, rights
Exam tip · Stakeholder analysis

For a case question, list 2–3 stakeholders, say whether each is internal or external, state what they want, and explain how they influence the decision (e.g., customers can boycott, governments can fine, employees can quit or strike).

Two CSR principles

Give back

Charity Principle

Wealthy firms have a duty to support the less fortunate through philanthropy and donations.

Examples: disaster relief, scholarships, food bank donations, medicines for clinics, paid volunteer days.

Take care of

Stewardship Principle

Leaders manage resources as caretakers for society, protecting the long-term health of people, communities and the environment.

Examples: sustainability investments, fair-wage supply chains, community infrastructure, replanting, employee development.

Exam tip · Charity vs. Stewardship

Charity = giving money or goods away (a one-way gift). Stewardship = how the business runs itself, managing resources responsibly for the long term. Donating to a flood fund is charity; switching to recycled packaging is stewardship.

Two views on social responsibility

Classical View (Milton Friedman)

Management's only responsibility is to maximize profits for shareholders. Friedman famously argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits.

Arguments against CSR: lower profits and higher costs; dilutes the business purpose; gives business too much social power; business lacks public accountability.

Socioeconomic View

Management must care about broader social welfare, creating value for society, not just profits.

Arguments for CSR: adds long-run profits; improves public image; avoids stricter government regulation; ethical obligation and better environment; meets public expectations.

Four CSR strategies: the spectrum

◀ Low responsibilityHigh responsibility ▶
ObstructionistActively avoids responsibility. Denies harm, hides information, fights laws in court, may act illegally.
DefensiveDoes the legal minimum. Admits errors only when forced by law or public pressure; apologises after backlash.
AccommodativeAccepts responsibility; meets economic, legal and ethical criteria; does what stakeholders request; e.g., funds clean-up after public concern.
ProactiveAnticipates issues and goes beyond the law to lead; e.g., Tesla's renewable energy, Microsoft's carbon-negative goal.
Exam tip · Accommodative vs. Proactive

Ask: who moved first? If the company acted after stakeholders asked or complained, it's Accommodative. If it acted before anyone asked, it's Proactive. Defensive = only because the law forced them.

CSR audits

A CSR (social responsibility) audit evaluates a company's social, ethical, environmental and legal performance to check whether it is meeting its responsibilities.

What it evaluates

  • Environmental practices (pollution, waste, energy)
  • Employee treatment (wages, safety, diversity, training)
  • Customer responsibility (product safety, truthful ads, privacy)
  • Community involvement (giving, volunteering)
  • Ethics and governance (corruption, transparency)
  • Legal compliance (labour, environmental, business law)

Why companies do it

  • Identify ethical or environmental problems
  • Improve decision-making
  • Build stakeholder trust
  • Protect reputation
  • Ensure legal compliance
  • Reduce future legal and financial risk
  • Improve long-term sustainability

Slide example: a clothing company's audit finds an overseas supplier with unsafe conditions. It changes suppliers, starts annual supplier inspections and publishes a sustainability report.

Lesson 6

Ethics, dilemmas and whistleblowing

Ethics

A code of moral principles that sets standards of good vs. bad, right vs. wrong.

Ethical behaviour

What is accepted as good and right within the governing moral code.

Exam tip · Ethics vs. Law

Legal ≠ ethical. Legal behaviour is not necessarily ethical, ethical behaviour is not necessarily legal, and the law sets a minimum standard; ethics can ask for more. Be ready to give an example of each.

The three brain teasers from class (legal or ethical?)
CaseLegal?Ethical?Principle

Values are broad beliefs about what is appropriate behaviour. They differ from person to person, which is why people respond differently to the same ethical challenge.

Three viewpoints on ethics across cultures

Cultural relativism

Right and wrong depend on the culture. No framework is superior.

A gift during negotiations: respect in some cultures, bribery in others.

Ethical universalism

Some principles (fairness, honesty) apply to all humans everywhere.

Child labour is wrong everywhere, whatever the local economy.

Ethical imperialism

Imposing your own culture's standards on others, ignoring local norms.

A U.S. firm enforces head-office dress code or a no-alcohol rule on all overseas staff.

Exam tip · Universalism vs. Imperialism

Both apply one standard everywhere. The difference: universalism applies basic human principles everyone shares (honesty, no child labour). Imperialism forces one culture's preferences (dress code, drinking rules, distribution methods) on another.

Ethical dilemmas

An ethical dilemma is a choice that offers personal or organizational benefit but may be unethical. Example: mining a remote village's gold while contaminating its water.

Dilemmas managers face

  • Discrimination (non-job-relevant bias)
  • Sexual harassment
  • Conflicts of interest: bribes and kickbacks
  • Customer confidence: sharing privileged information
  • Organizational resources: misusing supplies, money, email

Test of disclosure

Ask: "How would we feel if everyone knew about this decision?"

Not concerned → likely ethical. Uneasy → probably not.

Codes of ethics

Official written guidelines for situations prone to dilemmas: diversity, bribes and kickbacks, political contributions, honest records, customer/supplier relationships, confidentiality.

Exam tip · Bribe vs. Kickback

Bribe: a payment before the decision to influence it, usually one-time. "Here's money if you approve this deal."
Kickback: a cut of the proceeds paid after the deal is approved, often ongoing. "I'll give you 10% of the profits." A kickback is a type of bribe.

The 10-step checklist for ethical decisions
  1. Identify the ethical issue or problem.
  2. List the facts that directly affect the decision.
  3. Identify who might be affected, and how.
  4. Explain what each affected person would want you to do.
  5. Identify your options.
  6. Test each option: Is it legal? Is it ethical? Who does it benefit?
  7. Decide which option to follow.
  8. Double-check: "Would I be okay if my family found out?"
  9. Double-check: "Would I be okay reading about it in the newspaper?"
  10. Take the action.

Whistleblowing

A whistleblower exposes illegal, unethical or incorrect activity inside a private or public organization. Wrongdoing can include breaking policy or law, threats to the public interest, fraud or corruption. Whistleblowers were often vilified, but that view is shifting thanks to high-profile cases and laws that protect whistleblowers from retaliation.

Examples of protection in Canada (where BOH4M is taught): the Criminal Code makes it an offence for an employer to punish an employee for reporting a crime to law enforcement; the Public Servants Disclosure Protection Act protects federal public servants; Ontario's Occupational Health and Safety Act protects workers who raise safety concerns from reprisal. Your slides only require you to know that such laws exist and why.

Lesson 7

Globalization

Globalization is the process by which businesses operate and compete in international markets through increased trade, technology and communication, creating greater economic interdependence between countries. In plain words: products, services, ideas, money and workers cross borders more easily. Your phone may be designed in the USA, made in China and sold in Malaysia.

Advantages → manager impact

  • Market expansion (Apple, Samsung sell worldwide) → decide which countries to enter and how to adapt products
  • Access to resources and talent (IT support outsourced to India) → balance cost savings with quality control
  • Diverse workforce (global design teams) → lead multicultural teams
  • Economies of scale (Walmart, Shopee supply chains) → focus on efficiency and cost leadership

Disadvantages → manager impact

  • Cultural barriers (an ad that offends) → research local culture first
  • Increased competition (small shops vs. Amazon) → differentiate the business
  • Economic dependency (COVID-19 supply delays, sudden 50% tariffs) → plan risks and alternatives
  • Ethical and social issues (sweatshops) → ensure ethical decisions and comply with each country's laws
Exam tip · Exam practice from your slides

"Define globalization. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage." For full marks: definition + named advantage + real example + manager impact, then the same for a disadvantage.
